World Cup Trading Championships · 1983–2026

Winning is not edge

A contest that scores traders on the highest percentage return, over one year, on a real but deliberately tiny account. Forty-two years of champions. The question is what a win actually measures — and the answer is legible in the rulebook, in the mathematics of goal-reaching, and above all in what happened to the champions afterwards.

The rulebook

The leaderboard is a best-of-N draw, by rule

This is the finding that reframes everything else, and it is sitting in the entry agreement in plain language.

“Entrant may open more than one account at the beginning of the Championship or at any time during the Championship Period. Each account must be separately funded and margined… Only the best performing account will be used for ranking purposes.”

Rule 1, Official Futures Entry Agreement — replacing the pre-2021 language, which aggregated all of an entrant’s accounts

Rule 7 completes it: if equity falls below $2,500 the broker may liquidate, and “a new account may be established with new funds at any time.”

So one person can run five accounts, have four destroyed, and be ranked on the fifth. Nobody has cheated. The contest simply reports the maximum of a sample and calls it a result. And because only the top five are ever published, every failure in the entire history of the competition is invisible by construction — there is not one negative return anywhere in 416 rows of standings.

The entrant count has never been disclosed, in any year, in any document. That is the one number you would need to interpret the leaderboard at all.

In fairness, the mechanics are honest

The accounts are real, funded at CFTC-registered brokers. The denominator is cumulative deposits, withdrawals are added back so you cannot flatter the ratio by taking money out, all trading must happen inside the monitored account, and cross-account hedging is explicitly barred. The arithmetic is not rigged. What is misleading is not the sum — it is what the sum is taken to prove. The organiser says as much itself: “WCC competitors may control accounts that produce results substantially different than the results achieved in their WCC accounts.”

The mathematics

Contest-optimal bet size does not contain your edge

“Maximise the probability of reaching target b by time T” has a closed-form solution — Browne (1999), equivalently Föllmer–Leukert quantile hedging. The optimal dollar exposure is:

θ*(t,x) = b · φ( Φ⁻¹(x/b) ) / ( σ · √(T−t) ) ← contest-optimal θ_kelly = (λ/σ) · x ← growth-optimal λ = Sharpe ratio. It appears in one of these and not the other.

 Target 3× in one year, 30% volatility. Contest-optimal position size is identical across every level of skill, because it is the delta of a digital option — a hedging quantity, not an information quantity. Kelly scales directly with the edge.

Kelly sizing asks “how much do I know?” Contest sizing asks “how far am I from the bar, and how little time is left?” A trader with no edge sizes exactly like a trader with a large one. The edge changes their odds of winning; it does not change their bet.

And the payoff shape makes maximum variance rational rather than reckless. Your loss is capped at the stake and the fee; your win is a marketable track record. For an indicator payoff, every mean-preserving spread strictly raises your objective as long as you are not already expected to win. There is no interior optimum coming from the risk side at all.

The condition that makes it a trap

Convexity alone does not produce this. Panageas & Westerfield (2009) show that a manager with the same option-like payoff but an infinite horizon optimally behaves like a Kelly bettor. The risk-seeking “relies on combining finite horizons and convex compensation schemes rather than on convexity alone.” A tournament is convexity plus a hard 31 December. A career is convexity plus no deadline at all. Same payoff, opposite optimal policy — and the horizon is the switch.

 Turning a field from hopeless to world-class changes the champion’s headline by a factor of three — invisible on a leaderboard. It moves the median entrant from −45% to +56%. The leaderboard is the uninformative statistic; the distribution of all entrants is the informative one, and it is the one nobody publishes.

An independent extreme-value calculation reaches the same place analytically: for a zero-edge field the expected winning return is roughly N× capital, so any headline below ln N in log terms is fully explained by field size before you examine a single trade. Two separate routes put the probability of a 1987-magnitude result arising by chance in a 100-strong field at about 10% — and the contest has run every year since 1983.

That is not a claim that Larry Williams lacks skill; he has a multi-decade public record. It is a claim that the single headline number carries almost no evidential weight. Which is precisely what he says about it himself.

“For years people have asked for my trades to figure out how I did it. I gladly oblige them, they will learn little there — what created the gargantuan gain was not great trading ability nearly as much as the very aggressive form of money management I used… That’s what made the cool million smackers — not some great trading skill.”

Larry Williams, Long-Term Secrets to Short-Term Trading (Wiley, 1999)

In the same book he records the drawdown inside his winning year: the account ran from $10,000 to $2,100,000, then “spiraling down to $700,000” — a 66.7% peak-to-trough collapse — before finishing near $1.1m. He also records that the method destroyed accounts he ran for other people (“Brokers and clients screamed”), and that regulators seized his records for over a year. His prescription today is 10–15% of the account divided by the largest historical loss, with a warning that the cliff sits between 14% and 21%.

The platform’s own numbers

These are drawdowns on lead accounts that WorldCupAdvisor chose to feature, self-published and net of fees: Tim Birch −69.3%, Marco Vironda Gambin −63.3%, Petra Zacek (2018 champion) −56.5%, Stefano Serafini (2017 champion) −56.0%, Brent Carlile (2024 champion) −52.5%. One programme reached −78.2% and then disappeared from the rankings entirely.

The characteristic shape is a spectacular opening followed by a long give-back. Yuwen Cao’s programme reads +183% at 5 months, +235% at 12, +123% at 18, +55% at 33. Brent Carlile: +161% at 9 months, +70% at 16, +104% at 31, +39% at 37.

The tell, in one line

An independent CTA database ranks managers by year-to-date return. In November 2025 the number-one ranked programme was a World Cup Advisor account at +471.8% — with assets under management of $29,000. In the same table: Crabel Capital, +9.90% on $2.216 billion. Same period, same methodology. The return ranking is very close to an inverse ranking of account size, which is the entire phenomenon in a single table.

And the base rate for everyone else

US brokers must publish, quarterly, the share of retail forex accounts that were profitable. Across the three genuinely speculative brokers in Q2 2026 — 83,584 accounts — 33.5% were profitable. If quarters were independent, 1.2% would string four together.

The two best academic datasets say the same thing more sharply. Barber, Lee, Liu & Odean, on fifteen years of Taiwanese day traders: “less than 1% of the day trader population is able to predictably and reliably earn positive abnormal returns net of fees.” Roughly 20% look profitable in any given year — that 20% collapses to under 1% the moment you sort on one year and measure the next. Survival at one, two and three years: 44%, 24%, 15%.

And the futures-specific study, on Brazilian index futures, the world’s third-largest such market. Of 19,646 people who began day trading, 1,551 persisted for 300 days or more. Of those: 97% lost money, 0.4% earned more than a bank teller, and the single best individual made $310 a day with a standard deviation of $2,560. The authors found no evidence of learning.

What survives

The part that is real

The sceptical reading usually overshoots here, so state it plainly: Unger and Davey are genuinely systematic traders who have written down a replicable process. That process transfers. None of the headline numbers do, and both men say so in print.

  • Davey develops on one contract. He runs about fifty strategies simultaneously precisely so that no single one matters, and applies position sizing only afterwards, driven by Monte Carlo with hard caps on risk of ruin. His default assumption — that every backtest has good luck baked into it — is a multiple-testing correction expressed as a habit.
  • Unger diversifies across 30–40 markets and several system families, sizes by dollar-volatility bucketing, caps risk at 1% of equity in live trading against up to 20% in contests, and treats optimal f as a cliff to locate rather than a target to hit.
  • The asymmetry that justifies all of it. Expected log growth is symmetric about full Kelly, so half-Kelly and 1.5× Kelly compound at the same rate — but the probability of ever halving your capital goes from 12.5% to 79.4%, and of ever losing 90% from 0.1% to 46.4%. You buy a sixfold reduction in ruin risk for a quarter of your growth rate. Above 2× Kelly, growth is zero and ruin is certain while the arithmetic mean still explodes.
A warning specific to this tradition

Optimal f, as popularised in exactly these circles, sizes off the largest historical loss — an order statistic of the sample, and therefore the single most over-fitted quantity in any dataset. It is growth-optimal in-sample and systematically over-levered out-of-sample. In gappy markets that is worse than the lognormal model suggests, because ruin becomes an atom rather than a limit.

Verdict

Legitimate in its mechanics, misleading in its implications

Real trades, real money at registered brokers, an honest denominator, cross-account hedging barred, and one legal challenge to the business model that failed on the merits. None of that is in dispute.

Against it: an undisclosed field size, top-five-only publication so failures cannot be counted, an explicit rule that ranks your best account out of as many as you care to fund, no independent audit anywhere in the entry agreement, a percentage metric computed on a deliberately tiny stake, and an organiser that is simultaneously the introducing broker and a fee-splitting partner in the copy-trading platform the title feeds — where subscriptions can run 24–36% of the minimum account per year and lead traders are paid per follower, not on performance.

It manufactures marketable track records far more reliably than it identifies durable skill, and it does so without needing anyone to cheat.

The champion-to-educator pipeline is the dominant career path afterwards, and it is the honest tell: of the champions whose subsequent businesses can be traced, the largest ones sell instruction rather than returns.

The one-sentence answer

People win this contest by taking the largest position their nerve allows on the smallest account the rules permit, in a field whose size is never published, with only the best of their several accounts counted — and the mathematics says the optimal way to do that is identical whether or not they can trade at all.
